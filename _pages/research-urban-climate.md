Urban climate research investigates how cities alter the exchanges of heat, moisture, and momentum between the surface and atmosphere, generating distinct microclimates that influence weather, air quality, and human health. At its core, the **urban climate system** is governed by physical mechanisms such as radiation trapping within urban canyons, reduced evapotranspiration due to impervious surfaces, and the release of anthropogenic heat from traffic, buildings, and industry. These factors collectively create the **Urban Heat Island (UHI)** effect, where city centers remain warmer than surrounding rural areas, especially at night. Recent studies have expanded this framework by exploring how fine-scale processes—such as **traffic congestion**, **surface morphology**, and **energy infrastructure**—modulate the intensity and diurnal variability of the **Surface Urban Heat Island (SUHI)**. This integration of physical climatology and high-frequency urban data has revealed that human activity patterns are tightly coupled to thermal extremes, shaping both environmental exposure and resilience.

Parallel to this evolution, the concept of the **AI-enabled Urban Digital Twin (AIDT)** has emerged as a transformative direction in urban climate science:contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}. A digital twin represents a continuously updating virtual replica of the physical city, integrating real-time sensor data, physics-based simulations, and artificial intelligence to capture and predict system dynamics. In this context, AIDTs link high-resolution meteorological models with AI foundation models for Earth systems, enabling rapid, adaptive, and uncertainty-aware simulations. These models can assimilate observations from weather stations, satellite imagery, and urban sensor networks—such as flux towers and soil moisture probes—to monitor evolving atmospheric and surface conditions. The **bidirectional coupling** between the physical and virtual domains allows for real-time feedback, where observations refine the model while model outputs inform decisions on infrastructure management, heat mitigation, and hazard response. This fusion of physics-based reasoning with data-driven intelligence marks a pivotal step toward **operational, city-scale climate prediction systems**.

The integration of digital twins into urban climate science holds significant promise for advancing both scientific understanding and societal outcomes. By linking **multi-scale physical modeling** with **AI-enhanced analytics**, researchers can dynamically assess how urban form, vegetation, and human behavior interact to produce climate anomalies. Such systems enable scenario testing—evaluating how design interventions like cool roofs, green corridors, or transportation reforms influence local energy balance and air temperature. Beyond predictive accuracy, the AIDT framework emphasizes **transparency, adaptability, and stakeholder engagement**, allowing city planners and communities to explore actionable strategies for climate resilience. As cities become denser and more data-rich, the digital twin approach represents a new paradigm for **real-time environmental intelligence**, bridging physical processes and social systems to guide sustainable and equitable urban futures.
