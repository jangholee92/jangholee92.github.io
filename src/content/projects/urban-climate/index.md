Urban heat varies across a city and through the day. Using hourly GOES observations across 120 U.S. cities and field measurements in Chicago, I examine how vegetation, tree canopy, impervious surfaces, water, and traffic shape heat exposure. Chicago studies show that greenness and canopy can reduce heat while congestion adds a distinct, locally variable heat source. These findings help identify where cooling strategies should respond to neighborhood conditions rather than citywide averages.

Nature-based solutions require more than increasing tree cover. Research on urban tree traits shows how species respond to temperature and soil moisture, while work on plant selection and community co-design weighs cooling and other benefits against potential air-quality and allergy burdens. I bring satellite, ground, and ecological evidence together to inform planting choices and equitable heat adaptation.
